Design exploration · Foodsum · 31 Aug 2026

Photograph components, compose plates

A plate is a combination, and combinations explode: the 24 plates in the catalogue draw on just 31 distinct components, and those 31 can form 36,425 plates of two to four items. We are photographing the wrong noun. Generate one cutout per component, place them into katoris on a thali at ingest time, and any future combo is a composition instead of a generation run.

Raised by Sal: “where you could also place things on thali in case of meal… if you have chole in a katori you just need chole images and you can put it in thali, similarly rotis. It could help us generate more combos in future.” · Nothing here has been built or run.

The question

Is a meal image a photograph, or a composition?

Today every meal image is its own generated photograph of a whole arranged plate. That is one generation, one review, one ingest per combination. The alternative is to treat a meal image as a layout problem: photograph each component once in its own vessel, and compose the plate at ingest time onto a thali.

The decision is not “does compositing work” — sharp is already a dependency and does it. The decision is whether a composed plate is good enough to show a person as a picture of their dinner, and what has to change in the corpus for it to be possible at all.

1. The current corpus physically cannot be composited

STYLE.md’s fixed prefix specifies a “seamless flat light warm-grey background” with “a soft contact shadow under the vessel”, encoded as opaque WebP (scripts/ingest.mjs:227). Pasting one of those onto a thali pastes a grey rectangle with its own shadow. Composition needs a new image class with an alpha channel and no baked shadow — this is the real cost of the idea, not the code.

2. Plates are small, which is what makes layout tractable

Of the 24 plates: 14 have two components, 9 have three, 1 has four. A layout table covering 2–4 slots covers everything in the ledger, and a thali with more than five katoris is not a thali anybody plates at home.

3. The components are mostly the same handful of things

Ten of the catalogue’s grain dishes (roti, cooked-rice, naan, idli, bhature, masala-dosa…) and fourteen legume/vegetable dishes (dal, rajma, chana, aloo-sabzi, sambar…) account for nearly every plate. Roti and rice alone appear in more than half of them.

4. Nothing in the model knows what vessel a dish comes in

Dish (src/dishes.ts) carries slug, name, category, aliases, fromHealthFoodTable, note — and no notion of katori vs flatbread vs mound. category is close but wrong for this: grain holds both roti (flat, goes on the rim) and cooked-rice (a mound or a katori).

5. The output contract is a real raster, and must stay one

Each variant ships 12 rungs across three aspect ladders (4:3, 1:1, 16:9). Beacon renders images to 1-bit e-paper frames — 200×200 and 240×360 (Beacon/apps/mid/src/lib/frames.ts:13). Whatever we build has to end as an ordinary image file at an ordinary URL, or it works in Health and silently does nothing on the panel.

Recommendation

C, reached through B’s mechanics, and proven on one plate before anything is converted. Build the component class and the composer; generate cutouts only for the components the 16 outstanding plates need; compose those plates. Leave the 26 existing solo photographs exactly as they are — they work, and regenerating a working corpus to satisfy a symmetry is how quality gets lost.

Then the convergence is free rather than a migration: new dishes ship as components and get their solo view composed, while grandfathered dishes keep their photograph. Two classes coexist permanently and that is fine — it is the same shape as NoteBook’s plain-vs-HTML page bodies, where a boundary function decides which a thing is and both are legal for ever.

Runner-up: A. If a composed thali turns out to look uncanny — and that is a real possibility that only Sal’s eye can rule on — then photographing 16 plates is a day of work and the idea is closed cleanly. Option D is rejected outright, not on effort but because it silently excludes the device this suite was partly built for.

Mechanism

What a thali layout actually is

Three pieces, none of them clever: a base image, a table of slots, and a rule for choosing which slot a dish may occupy.

The vessel field

A new optional field on Dish, because category cannot answer this: katori (anything wet or loose — dal, chole, sabzi, raita, chutney), flat (roti, naan, dosa, bhature — laid on the rim), mound (rice, poha — a heap on the tray itself), whole (a thing that is its own plate — a sandwich, a shake, yogurt in its own bowl).

A whole dish disqualifies a plate from composition. A whey shake does not go on a thali, and whey-banana-shake should stay a photograph. That is a feature: the vessel field is also how a plate opts out.

Choosing a layout

Keyed by (count of katori items, has flat, has mound). With 14 two-item and 9 three-item plates, the whole table is perhaps six entries. Slots fill in the meal’s declared component order, so composition is deterministic — the same plate composes identically every time, which is what lets the result be cached and content-hashed like any other variant.

The plan

Five steps, cheapest and most decisive first

  1. Prove it looks right, before building anything. Add a component prefix to STYLE.md (one katori, transparent background, no shadow, fixed vessel diameter, 90° overhead, filling the frame). Generate three cutouts — chana, roti, gulab-jamun — and hand-composite one chole-bhature thali. Done when Sal looks at it and says yes or no. Everything below is wasted if this step fails, which is why it is first and why it involves no code.
  2. The thali base and slot table. One base asset plus a small layout table. Done when a JSON layout renders empty slots at the right positions.
  3. vessel on Dish, and a plate’s composability derived from it. Additive and backwards-compatible; absent means whole, so nothing changes until a dish opts in. Done when npm run brief reports which plates are composable and which need a photograph.
  4. composeMeal() in the ingest pipeline. Writes into corpus/images/<meal-slug>/ exactly like an ingested photograph, through the same 12-rung ladder and the same budget check — so nothing downstream learns a new URL shape, and Beacon gets a real raster. Done when a composed plate passes npm run check unmodified.
  5. Backfill. Generate cutouts for the components the 16 outstanding plates need, compose them, review as a contact sheet. Done when npm run missing reports zero plates.

Steps 1–3 are independently useful even if step 4 is never built: the vessel field improves prompts on its own, since a prompt that names the vessel produces a more consistent photograph.

Deliberately out of scope

Doors left shut, and why

  • noRegenerating the 26 existing dish photographs as cutouts. They work. A symmetry that costs a working corpus is not worth having.
  • noPerspective or 3D plating. Everything in this corpus is 90° overhead precisely so that scale and framing are comparable. A tilted thali reintroduces every problem flat-lay was chosen to avoid.
  • noRegional thali variants (banana leaf, steel compartment tray). One tray until there is a reason for two.
  • noComposition at request time. Composed variants are ingested and committed like any other image, so the CDN, the tag-based publish flow and Beacon’s poll all work unchanged.
  • keepPhotographed plates stay first in precedence. A real photograph of a real plate beats a composition; composition fills the gap, it does not replace the top tier.

Open questions — Sal’s call

Four things I cannot decide from the code

Does a composed thali actually look like food?

This is the whole risk and no amount of design settles it. Separately generated components will differ slightly in scale, white balance and how full the vessel is; composited, that can read as a collage. Step 1 exists solely to answer this, and if the answer is no, we photograph 16 plates and close the idea.

Where does the thali itself come from — generated, or drawn?

A generated photograph of an empty thali is realistic but fixes its own lighting and perspective for ever. A drawn/vector tray composites perfectly and may look flat next to photographic food. My instinct is a generated tray, because the food is photographic and the tray must match it — but I would rather see both once.

Does a composed image survive 1-bit e-paper?

Beacon dithers to pure black and white at 200×200. Cutout edges and soft composited shadows are exactly what dithering handles worst. Checkable cheaply once one composition exists, and worth checking before the backfill rather than after.

Should the solo dish view eventually compose too?

That is the difference between “plates get cheaper” and “everything gets cheaper”, and it is what makes every future dish plate-ready automatically. It only becomes safe once a composed solo shot has been compared side by side with a photographed one.

Verification status

Nothing in this plan has been built or run. No cutout has been generated, no thali composed, no code written. What was executed today: the catalogue counts (24 plates, 8 with images, 16 without, 46 dishes, 31 distinct components), the combinatorial figure, the plate-size distribution, the aspect/rung ladders, the presence of sharp 0.34.5 with composite(), the absence of any vessel field on Dish, and Beacon’s 1-bit frame geometry — all read from the repo or computed from corpus/index.json.

The claim I am least sure of is the one the whole idea rests on: that separately generated components composite into something that reads as a meal. I have not seen it done in this style and I would not bet the backfill on it. Hence step 1.
